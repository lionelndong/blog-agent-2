# Chapter 2: Triggers

## Core Idea
Triggers are stimuli in the environment that prompt people to think about related things. Because people talk about whatever is top of mind, the more frequently something is triggered, the more it gets talked about. **Top of mind means tip of tongue.**

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Triggers**: environmental cues that make a product/idea accessible (top of mind), which leads to talk and action.
- **Immediate vs. ongoing word of mouth**:
  - *Immediate* — talk soon after an experience (interesting things win here).
  - *Ongoing* — talk over weeks/months (triggers win here; interest does NOT sustain talk).
  - When to use: pick based on whether you need a launch spike (movies, new food) or sustained talk (campaigns, policy).
- **Grow the habitat**: create new links between your product and frequent, prevalent cues in the environment (Kit Kat ↔ coffee).
  - How: find a stimulus your audience encounters often, then repeatedly pair your product with it.
- **The poison parasite**: make a rival's message a trigger for *your* message (antismoking ad spoofing Marlboro: "Bob, I've got emphysema").

## Key Concepts
- **Accessibility / top of mind**: how readily a thought comes to mind; driven by chronic interest or by environmental stimuli.
- **Habitat**: the set of triggers that cause people to think about a product (hot dogs: barbecues, summer, baseball, dachshunds).
- **Frequency vs. strength trade-off**: frequent triggers help, but a cue linked to *many* things is a weak trigger for any one (the "holes in a paper cup" analogy; "red" triggers nothing strongly).
- **Proximity to behavior**: the best trigger fires *near where/when the desired action happens* (bath-mat ad fails — you can't buy a mat in the shower; "Man Drinks Fat" fires when you reach for soda).

## Mental Models
- **Consider the context**: design your message to be triggered by your audience's everyday environment, not just to be clever.
- A strong trigger beats a catchy slogan: the "corny" *tray* fruit-and-veggie slogan changed behavior; the clever "live healthy" one didn't.
- Even **negative attention helps if it triggers** awareness (negative reviews raised sales 45% for unknown authors; "stinky socks" wine; Shake Weight).

## Anti-patterns
- **Going for "interesting" over triggered**: interesting earns a burst, not sustained talk (the pirate costume vs. Ziploc bags).
- **Linking to an infrequent cue**: "Holidays are made for Michelob" failed; "Weekends are made for Michelob" worked (weekends recur weekly).
- **Triggers far from the action**: clever but misplaced cues (GEICO caveman — rarely seen; campus health-center drinking posters far from frat houses).

## Worked Example
**Kit Kat and coffee.** By 2007 Kit Kat sales were falling ~5%/yr and there was no TV budget. Colleen Chorak researched *when* people ate Kit Kats: often on a break, often with a hot beverage. The campaign — "a break's best friend" — repeatedly paired Kit Kat with coffee. Why coffee and not "Kit Kat and cantaloupe" (also alliterative)? Because **coffee is a frequent environmental stimulus** — drunk many times daily, year-round (vs. seasonal hot chocolate). Sales rose 8% in a year, up a third in twelve months; the brand grew from ~$300M to ~$500M.

**Mars bar / NASA Pathfinder (1997).** Mars bar sales rose with zero marketing change — because NASA's *Pathfinder* mission to *Mars* dominated the news, triggering the candy. ("Perhaps Sunny Delight should send NASA to the sun.")

## Key Takeaways
1. **Top of mind → tip of tongue**: frequently triggered things get talked about more, and *keep* getting talked about.
2. Distinguish **immediate vs. ongoing** WOM and design for the one you need.
3. **Consider the context** — link to cues common in your audience's environment.
4. **Grow the habitat** by pairing your product with a *frequent* stimulus.
5. Place triggers **close to the desired behavior** in time and place.
