# Introduction: Why Things Catch On

## Core Idea
Word of mouth — not inherent interest, luck, or a handful of "influentials" — is what makes products, ideas, and behaviors spread. Virality isn't born, it's made: any product or idea can be engineered to be more contagious by building in the right ingredients.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **STEPPS (the six principles of contagiousness)**: the book's organizing framework — **S**ocial Currency, **T**riggers, **E**motion, **P**ublic, **P**ractical Value, **S**tories. Each chapter is one principle.
  - When to use: any time you want a product, message, or behavior to spread by word of mouth.
  - How: diagnose and design for one or more STEPPS; you don't need all six — treat them like salad toppings, not a strict recipe.
- **Message over messenger**: stop hunting for special people to spread your idea; focus on making the idea itself worth spreading.
  - When to use: before spending on "influencer" outreach.
  - How: ask "what about this message makes people want to pass it on?" not "who has the most followers?"

## Key Concepts
- **Social transmission / word of mouth**: social influence and sharing; the primary factor behind 20–50% of purchasing decisions.
- **Contagious**: likely to spread — to diffuse person to person via word of mouth and social influence.
- **Social epidemic**: products, ideas, behaviors diffusing through a population like a virus.
- **The 7% rule**: only ~7% of word of mouth happens online (Keller Fay Group); offline conversation is more prevalent and often more impactful.
- **Virality is made, not born**: even "boring" products can be engineered to be contagious.

## Mental Models
- Think of word of mouth as **more persuasive and more targeted than advertising** — friends are candid (credible) and naturally route info to interested people, so it's ~10x more effective than traditional ads.
- Think of contagion like **a great joke**: so inherently good it spreads regardless of who tells it. Design the joke, don't just find good tellers.
- Treat **social media as technology, not strategy** — a Facebook page or tweet only works if people actually have a reason to talk.

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing "influentials"**: betting your spread on a few special people; in most cases they aren't meaningfully more effective at making things go viral.
- **Over-indexing on online**: assuming ~50% of WOM is online when it's ~7%; you neglect the larger offline channel.
- **"Just be interesting"**: interest alone is a weak, incomplete theory (see Triggers) — many funny/cute things never spread.
- **Confusing observation with cause**: noting that viral hits are funny while ignoring the funny content that flopped (the "Bill Clinton/Gates/Cosby → change your name to Bill" fallacy).

## Worked Example
**Barclay Prime's $100 cheesesteak.** Howard Wein needed buzz for a new luxury Philadelphia steakhouse with no big ad budget. He took the humble $4 cheesesteak and rebuilt it with Kobe beef, lobster, truffles, foie gras, and a split of Veuve Clicquot — priced at $100. People rushed to tell others ("absurd story-telling rights"); USA Today, the WSJ, Letterman, and Discovery covered it. It worked because it stacked STEPPS: remarkable (Social Currency), surprising (Emotion), useful signal of quality (Practical Value), a great narrative (Story), and frequently triggered by cheesesteaks in Philadelphia (Triggers). The restaurant flourished against long odds.

**Will It Blend? (Blendtec).** A "boring old blender." Marketer George Wright spent a $50 budget on marbles, golf balls, a rake, and a lab coat, and filmed founder Tom Dickson blending them. First-week views: 6 million; the series passed 300M views; retail sales rose 700% in two years — proof that virality is made, not born.

## Key Takeaways
1. Focus on the **message**, not the messenger — make the thing inherently worth talking about.
2. **Any** product or idea can be made contagious; "boring" is not a verdict, it's a design problem.
3. WOM beats ads because it's **credible and targeted** — and most of it happens offline.
4. Use **STEPPS** as a checklist; even one or two principles can drive a product to catch on.
5. Don't confuse a few viral hits' features with their causes — study failures too.
