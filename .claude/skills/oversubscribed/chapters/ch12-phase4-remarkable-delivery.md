# Chapter 12 (Phase 4 of the CDE Method): Remarkable Delivery

## Core Idea
"This is the most important step in the process by far." Build-up creates expectation; if delivery doesn't beat it, the energy turns against you. **Remarkable = "worthy of being talked about."** Make *every* touchpoint remarkable and leave people **uplifted**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Positively Remarkable Delivery**: "Sell the sizzle not the steak" is over — "if the 'steak' is no good, the business is over." A dissatisfied customer once told 11 people; now they tell thousands, and reviews get indexed on Google for years. "Your marketing team is comprised of your clients."
- **The Remarkable Audit**: list *every* touchpoint (website, brochures, people, products, premises, ads, staff handbook, uniforms, packaging — for customers, team, and suppliers) and score each **1–10** on "Is this positively remarkable?" Then improve 1–2 per month. The fastest-growing companies spend **5–15% of revenue** on research, development, innovation and training — not on ads.
- **Energy Up, Down or Sideways**: emotions are "little storms of energy."
  - **Up** — delight, surprise, unexpected caring (positive experiences you didn't expect).
  - **Down** — disappointment, anger, resentment (negative experiences not communicated in advance).
  - **Sideways** — satisfaction, indifference (a job done as expected). *Only "up" gets people talking.*
- **The 70% Rule (under-promise, keep magic up your sleeve)**: "When you sell, only talk about 70% of what you intend on doing." Keep some deliverable good stuff secret so the surprise creates *up* energy. (Penn & Teller's unannounced street-magic show after the theatre.)
- **From Now On, You're Also an IT Business**: "'We're not very techie' is the sound businesses make before they die." You can't scale remarkable delivery without systems (CRM, delivery tracking, follow-up automation).

## Key Concepts
- **Remarkable means the whole business**: "Don't just think about one aspect… the whole lot has to be positively remarkable."
- **The remarkable test (sample questions)**: Would a customer link to your blog? Tweet about your service? Photograph your product for Facebook? Would a competitor say they want to be like you? Would the newspapers write a positive story?
- **The discomfort is your friend**: how much there is to fix "is keeping the 'wannabes' and posers out of your league." "If creating value were straightforward, it wouldn't be valuable."

## Mental Models
- **The best product wins**: "If you commit to build the best product and keep investing in it, you're playing a winning strategy."
- Surprise math: a Porsche promised in 3 days and arriving on day 4 disappoints; promised in 6 weeks and arriving in 4 makes you ecstatic — same car, different expectation gap.

## Worked Example
**Penn & Teller's encore on the pavement:** Priestley enjoyed the London show exactly as expected (sideways → satisfied). Then outside, the magicians performed unannounced street magic in two crowds — photos, disappearing objects, laughter. "It was the highlight of the evening." They didn't need to; everyone was already satisfied. By keeping it a surprise (had it been advertised, some would've been annoyed about poor spots), they converted satisfaction into *up* energy and word of mouth.

## Anti-patterns
- **Meeting expectations exactly (sideways energy)**: "they won't talk about you and all your efforts… will have been for nothing."
- **Announcing all your good stuff up front**: removes the surprise that creates delight.
- **Refusing to invest in technology/systems**: you can't scale remarkable delivery, and you overpay for staff/software you don't need.

## Key Takeaways
1. Delivery is the most important phase — it must exceed the expectation build-up created.
2. Audit every touchpoint 1–10; improve 1–2 a month; invest 5–15% of revenue in the product.
3. Aim for *up* energy: deliver something great people didn't see coming.
4. Sell ~70% of what you'll deliver; keep magic in reserve. And become an IT business.
