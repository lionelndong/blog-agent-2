# Chapter 6 (Principle 5): It's OK to be Different

## Core Idea
"If you do what others do, you'll get what others get." Becoming oversubscribed requires a clear **philosophy** and the courage to fail, say no, make people wait, and buck the trend — polarizing the market on purpose.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Power of Philosophy**: your strongly held beliefs about what you do. It polarizes — "the more he talks about his beliefs… the more people become his loyal fans" (Pete Evans on food). "If you have no philosophy you appear to be bland… You're back to competing on price."
  - The four philosophy questions: *What do you stand for? What do you stand against? What are your strongly held beliefs? What change do you want to see in the world?*
- **It's OK to Fail**: being visibly "unable to cope with demand" creates mystique (Twitter's "fail whale" signalled the platform was oversubscribed). Be honest about being fully booked.
- **It's OK to say "No"**: "'No' requires you to develop high standards and stick to them." (Studio 54 turned people away; Google's ~1m applicants for a few thousand jobs; Netflix pays $2,000 to quit.) "Saying no to someone who's not right… creates the space for perfect clients to show up."
- **It's OK to Make People Wait**: waiting builds hunger and signals worth (Steve Jobs made people wait for info, release date, and outside the store; Granger & Co's breakfast queue). "Making people wait sends a message that what you have is worth waiting for."
- **It's OK to Buck the Trend**: play a different game (Tremesso, the inland Lake Como pizzeria with plastic chairs and one menu item, full every night). "It's OK to be old-fashioned when everyone is modern; expensive when everyone is cheap; flamboyant when everyone fits in."

## Mental Models
- **Polarize on purpose**: "The truer he remains to his philosophy, the more fans he attracts — and the more detractors he deals with." Focus on those who agree, not those who don't.
- **The customer is *not* always king**: oversubscribed businesses "have something of high value and they can only deliver it properly on certain terms that work for both parties."
- **Built-in radar**: you already feel when to say no; "the problems come when you ignore it."

## Worked Example
**The burned-out business coach:** Priestley called a highly recommended coach, who took a week to reply and said he was taking six weeks off because he was burned out and to call back then. Priestley's first reaction was skeptical ("Shouldn't he have systems?") — yet he set a reminder, called back, and the coach then *interviewed him*, handed over a list of rules, gave a year's holiday dates in advance, and seemed "unfazed by the idea of walking away from my money." Result: Priestley signed a $30,000 programme. Naming terms and being willing to walk away increased desire.

## Anti-patterns
- **Being "boring, plain and beige," trying to please everyone**: "attempting to fit in with the crowd will get you nothing more than the crowd."
- **Squeezing people in at the expense of the experience**: "a short-term approach that leads to a long-term struggle."

## Key Takeaways
1. Develop and broadcast a clear philosophy; let it polarize.
2. It's OK to fail, say no, make people wait, and buck the trend — each signals high value.
3. Set high standards and terms; turning the wrong people away makes room for the right ones.
4. Don't sacrifice quality to fit one more customer through the door.
