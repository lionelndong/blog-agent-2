# Chapter 2 (Principle 1): Demand and Supply Set the Price

## Core Idea
Price and profit are set by the balance of demand and supply — and you can **make your own market forces** rather than accept the market's. "Markets go up because there are more buyers than sellers… and that's it."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Story of the Two Bidders**: at an auction it only takes *two* people who want the one thing to push the price up indefinitely. "Two people who desire something is enough to oversubscribe the one person who has it." (Gary Vaynerchuk's charity consult → £8,000; Facebook vs Google bidding WhatsApp to $19bn.)
  - When to use: to stop obsessing over the whole market's price.
  - How: find the small group who value you most and serve them better than anyone.
- **Profits, Losses or Wages? (the three states)**: the only thing that matters is the demand:supply relationship, even for the same product.
  - **Oversubscribed** — demand outstrips supply → *profit* tolerated on top of wages.
  - **Balanced** — demand ≈ supply → normal *wages* tolerated, but not profit.
  - **Undersubscribed** — excess supply → *losses*.
- **The market abhors a profit**: "A profit is only tolerated if demand is higher than supply." No one but your stakeholders wants you to be profitable, so you only profit when oversubscribed on capacity.

## Key Concepts
- **Make your own market forces** — you can become "a market on your own," independent of economy and industry.
- **Some people must miss out**: being oversubscribed *requires* that some willing buyers don't get it. "Lots of people want a Ferrari — but the people at Ferrari aren't losing sleep over it."
- **Three layers of demand/supply**: the whole *economy* (e.g. the Roaring Twenties), your *industry* (e.g. the dot-com boom), and *you* as your own market.

## Mental Models
- Your value is much higher than you think *to a small number of people*. You don't need everyone — just enough people to drive your price up.
- If you focus on the wider-market price, "you'll always be average."

## Worked Example
**The plastic surgeons of LA:** In 1980s California, demand for cosmetic surgery vastly exceeded the supply of surgeons — they made fortunes (mansions, yachts, 10 cars). Attracted by the money, medical students flooded in; by the late '90s supply caught demand, the relationship rebalanced, and most LA plastic surgeons now earn a normal surgeon's wage. *Same product, changed ratio, changed profit* — proof that profitability tracks the demand:supply balance, not the product itself.

## Anti-patterns
- **Focusing on the entire marketplace**: worrying what the *majority* will pay instead of finding the few who really value you. "If you focus on the wider market price, you'll always be average."
- **Trying to sell to everyone**: the very mindset of not wanting anyone to miss out prevents you from becoming oversubscribed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Price is a *result* of being oversubscribed or not — it isn't fixed by the market.
2. You only make a profit when demand exceeds your capacity to supply.
3. Get comfortable with some willing buyers missing out — that's the mechanism.
4. You can separate from both economy and industry and become your own market.
